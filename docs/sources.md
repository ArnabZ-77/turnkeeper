## Background evidence (not for on-screen numbers)

- Williams College Economics working paper on a South Indian chit lender ([PDF](https://web.williams.edu/Economics/wp/raicosign_kr.pdf)): the organizer bears the default risk and requires winners to pledge cosigners, and the cosigners themselves encourage repayment. It backs "someone is always the bank" (the organizer). Paraphrase only.
- McNabb, LeMay-Boucher & Bonan, enforcement in ROSCAs, urban Benin field survey (*Eur. J. Dev. Res.*, 2019; [preprint](https://re.public.polimi.it/bitstream/11311/1100012/1/Enforcement_Roscas_EJDR_Feb2019_pre-print.pdf)): screening new members and repeated interaction reduce enforcement problems. This parallels the charter's collateral rule (no track record, no early pot).
- Anderson, Baland & Moene, "Enforcement in informal saving groups", *J. Dev. Econ.* 90(1), 2009: 620 groups incl. 385 ROSCAs surveyed in Kibera, Nairobi (1997). "Money problems are clearly the most common problem these groups face"; about 9% of groups used expulsion; one group lost 11 of 36 members who could not keep up contributions. [Working paper PDF](https://sv.uio.no/econ/english/research/publications/Memoranda/working-papers/pdf-files/2003/Memo-17-2003.pdf)
- NYC Department of Consumer Affairs, *Immigrant Financial Services Study* (2013): tandas are "a common tool among Mexican immigrants in New York City for managing informal savings"; "informal savings are also risky, and often require significant time to manage." [PDF](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/partners/Research-ImmigrantFinancialStudy-FullReport.pdf)
- IFMR via Business Standard (2010): default rate in registered chit funds "as low as one to two per cent". [J-PAL copy](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/Unregistered%20chit%20funds.pdf)
